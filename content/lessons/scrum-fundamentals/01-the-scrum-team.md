## Learning objective {#objective}
By the end of this lesson you will be able to:

- describe the three accountabilities in a Scrum Team
- explain how a Scrum Master serves the team, the Product Owner and the organisation
- name the five Scrum values and recognise them in action

This lesson follows the Scrum Guide (2020). Anything that isn't in the Guide is labelled "Common workplace practice (optional)". It takes about 18 minutes.

## Explanation {#explanation}
### The Scrum Team
A **[[Scrum Team]]** is small – typically 10 or fewer people – and has no sub-teams or hierarchy. It is:

- **[[cross-functional|Cross-functional]]:** together, the members have all the skills needed to create something valuable every Sprint
- **[[self-managing|Self-managing]]:** the team decides who does what, when and how

The Scrum Guide describes three **[[accountability|accountabilities]]**. They are not job titles or ranks – they describe what each person is answerable for.

### The Product Owner
The **[[Product Owner]]** is accountable for maximising the value of the product. That includes managing the Product Backlog: setting out the Product Goal, creating and explaining backlog items, ordering them, and making sure the backlog is visible and understood. The Product Owner is one person, not a committee. Others can ask for changes, but they do it by talking to the Product Owner.

### The Developers
The **[[Developers (in Scrum)|Developers]]** are everyone in the Scrum Team who creates the work in each Sprint – people who write code, test, design and more. They create the plan for the Sprint, keep quality high by meeting the Definition of Done, adapt their plan every day, and hold each other accountable as professionals.

### The Scrum Master
The **[[Scrum Master]]** is accountable for establishing Scrum as the Scrum Guide describes it, and for the Scrum Team's effectiveness. The Guide calls Scrum Masters *true leaders who serve the Scrum Team and the larger organisation*.

A Scrum Master serves:

- **the team** – by coaching self-management, helping the team focus on valuable, finished work, causing **[[impediment|impediments]]** to be removed, and making sure the Scrum events happen and are positive, productive and kept within their time limits
- **the Product Owner** – by helping find ways to define the Product Goal and manage the backlog, and helping the team understand why clear backlog items matter
- **the organisation** – by leading, training and coaching people in using Scrum, and removing barriers between the team and the people it works with

What a Scrum Master is **not**: the team's project manager, the person who assigns tasks, or the meeting secretary. Telling people what to do would undermine self-management.

### The Scrum values
Scrum works when people live five **[[Scrum values]]**: **commitment** (to the team's goals and each other), **focus** (on the work of the Sprint), **openness** (about the work and its challenges), **respect** (for each other as capable people) and **courage** (to do the right thing and work on hard problems).

## At ShopEasy {#workplace-example}
ShopEasy's checkout team has a Product Owner, Priya; a Scrum Master, Tom; and five Developers, including a tester.

On Monday a sales manager, Dev, walks up to a developer and asks her to "quickly add a banner for the weekend sale". The developer isn't sure what to do and asks Tom.

Tom doesn't decide whether the banner gets built – that isn't his accountability. He also doesn't tell the developer to drop her work. Instead he:

1. thanks Dev and explains that new requests go to Priya, who orders the Product Backlog
2. helps Dev and Priya talk it through that afternoon
3. later coaches the team on how to handle requests like this themselves next time

Priya decides the banner can wait until the next Sprint, because adding it now would put the current Sprint Goal at risk. Dev isn't thrilled, but he understands why. Tom showed **respect** to everyone and **courage** in sending a manager's request to the Product Owner instead of quietly agreeing to it.

## Worked example {#worked-example}
Here are four things people asked ShopEasy's Scrum Master to do. Let's decide whether each fits the accountability.

| Request | Fits? | Why |
|---|---|---|
| "Tell Sam to work on the payments bug today." | No | The Developers decide who does what. Tom could help the team talk about priorities instead. |
| "Make sure the retrospective happens – the team keeps skipping it." | Yes | Making sure Scrum events happen and are useful is part of the role. |
| "Take the minutes of every meeting." | No | Note-taking isn't the role. The team can agree how it records decisions. |
| "Help the new Product Owner learn to order the backlog." | Yes | Serving the Product Owner includes helping with backlog management. |

A useful question to ask: *Does this help the team and organisation use Scrum well and become more effective – or does it take decisions away from the people accountable for them?*

## Guided practice {#guided-practice}
A team member says: "The test environment keeps crashing and we lose half a day each time. I've asked IT twice and nothing happens."

What kind of thing is this, and what might the Scrum Master do?

:::hint
Is this something slowing the team down that the team can't easily fix on its own?
:::

:::hint
It's an impediment. The Scrum Master doesn't have to fix it personally, but should cause it to be removed – for example by raising it with the IT manager, or helping the team find who can fix it, and making the cost visible.
:::

Which Scrum value does the team member show by raising it?

:::hint
They're being honest about a problem that's hurting the work. That's openness.
:::
