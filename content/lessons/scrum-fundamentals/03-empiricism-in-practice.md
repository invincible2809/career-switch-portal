## Explanation {#explanation}
### Empiricism
Scrum is founded on **[[empiricism]]** and **[[lean thinking]]**. Empiricism means knowledge comes from experience, and decisions are based on what we can observe. Instead of trying to predict everything at the start, the team works in short cycles and learns as it goes.

Empiricism rests on three pillars:

- **[[Transparency]]** – the work and the process are visible to everyone involved. If the team hides problems, decisions will be based on a false picture.
- **[[Inspection]]** – the artifacts and progress towards goals are checked often and carefully, so problems are spotted early.
- **[[Adaptation]]** – when something is off course, the team adjusts as soon as possible.

The three depend on each other. Inspection without transparency is misleading. Inspection without adaptation is pointless.

Every Scrum event is an opportunity to inspect and adapt: progress towards the Sprint Goal at the Daily Scrum, the Increment at the Sprint Review, and the way of working at the Retrospective.

### When things go wrong
Problems in a Scrum Team are normal – they are information. A Scrum Master's job is to help make them visible and help the team and organisation respond, not to cover them up or fix everything personally.

Some common patterns and what they might suggest:

| What you notice | What it might suggest |
|---|---|
| Work regularly "spills over" into the next Sprint | Too much taken on, items too big, or the Definition of Done is unclear |
| The Daily Scrum is a status report to one person | The team isn't yet self-managing; the purpose of the event isn't understood |
| Stakeholders go straight to Developers with urgent requests | The Product Owner's accountability isn't understood or respected |
| The Retrospective produces the same complaints every time | Actions aren't specific, owned or followed up |

### Helpful Scrum Master responses
Good responses usually:

- **make things visible** – for example, bring data about unfinished work to the Retrospective
- **ask questions rather than give answers** – "What's getting in the way of finishing?" helps the team think
- **send decisions to the people accountable for them** – requests go to the Product Owner, technical decisions stay with the Developers
- **remove barriers outside the team's control** – by talking to managers or other teams

Unhelpful responses take decisions away from people: assigning tasks, promising stakeholders that work will be squeezed in, or quietly extending the Sprint.

## At ShopEasy {#workplace-example}
For three Sprints in a row, the checkout team has finished only about half of what it planned. Unfinished items roll over each time.

Tom, the Scrum Master, doesn't tell the team to work harder. He:

1. **makes it transparent:** he puts a simple chart on the team's wall showing items planned and items done for the last three Sprints (*Common workplace practice (optional)* – the Scrum Guide doesn't prescribe charts)
2. **helps the team inspect it** at the Retrospective, asking: "What do you notice? What's making items hard to finish?"
3. **lets the team adapt:** they realise several items were huge and only "almost done" at the end. They agree to split large items during refinement, and to finish one before starting another

At the next Sprint Planning, Priya and the Developers choose fewer, smaller items. Two Sprints later, most of what's planned gets done – and meets the Definition of Done.

## Worked example {#worked-example}
**Situation:** in the middle of a Sprint, the head of sales emails two Developers directly: "I need the gift-card feature live by Friday for a big client. Drop what you're doing."

Let's compare responses.

- **Unhelpful – Tom tells the Developers to do it.** He has taken a product decision that belongs to the Product Owner, and the Sprint Goal is now at risk without anyone deciding that's acceptable.
- **Unhelpful – Tom tells the head of sales "no, Scrum doesn't allow that".** This is unhelpful and not quite true. It also damages the relationship.
- **Helpful – Tom thanks the head of sales, explains that Priya orders the work, and sets up a quick conversation between them.** He makes the trade-off transparent: doing this now would put the Sprint Goal at risk. Priya decides. If the gift card really is more valuable, she might put it at the top of the Product Backlog for the next Sprint – or, if it makes the current Sprint Goal obsolete, consider cancelling the Sprint.

Afterwards, Tom coaches the Developers on how to respond to direct requests themselves: "Thanks – let's take this to Priya."

## Guided practice {#guided-practice}
At every Retrospective, the team says "the test environment is slow". Nothing changes.

What might Tom try?

:::hint
Think about what makes an improvement actually happen: who owns it, and what exactly will be done?
:::

:::hint
Help the team agree one specific action with an owner – for example, "Asha will book 30 minutes with the infrastructure lead this week to show how much time we lose." If it's outside the team's control, Tom can help remove the barrier with the people who can change it.
:::

Which pillar of empiricism is missing if, at the Sprint Review, the team says everything planned is finished, even though several items don't meet the Definition of Done?

:::hint
If stakeholders can't see what's really happening, they're deciding on a false picture.
:::
